Semantic segmentation assigns predefined labels to every pixel in an image and is crucial for detailed visual analysis. However, deploying Deep Neural Networks(DNNs) for semantic segmentation in safety-critical applications is challenging due to their tendency to be overconfident, particularly with Out of Distribution (OOD) data, leading to unreliable predictions and potential risks in domains like autonomous driving. Uncertainty estimation is, therefore, crucial for ensuring the reliability and robustness of semantic segmentation models. While existing models often report predictive uncertainty during inference, they typically do not leverage this knowledge during training. To bridge this gap, our thesis explores leveraging various uncertainty estimation techniques as guiding signals during the training of semantic segmentation models with the aim of improving segmentation performance, reliability, and robustness. We investigate uncertainty estimation techniques such as Monte Carlo Dropout (MCD), Online Hard Example Mining (OHEM), and Evidential Deep Learning (EDL), along with their finetuned variants, and comprehensively analyzed their impact on segmentation performance, OOD detection, and model calibration. Extensive experiments on the three standard benchmark datasets, Cityscapes, Fishyscapes, and LostandFound, reveal nuanced trade-offs between uncertainty estimation methods. While some models showed limited improvement in segmentation performance compared to the conventional baseline, EDL based models consistently outperformed all approaches in distinguishing between In-distribution (ID) and OOD samples, highlighting their superior ability to represent and leverage uncertainty information. A key contribution of this work lies in demonstrating the significant impact of uncertainty guidance training on model calibration and OOD detection. Despite these advantages, the trade-offs between segmentation accuracy and OOD detection highlights the need for further research on optimizing uncertainty integration, possibly through adaptive uncertainty modulation or novel loss functions.
